Agriculture

Agriculture in Kerala: An overview

Agriculture remains a major pillar for Kerala’s social and economic fabric. Kerala agriculture is largely a mix of homestead farming, high-value plantation crops, and small- and medium-scale landholding agriculture1. The sector is increasingly unable to cope with climate-induced stress, including rising temperatures. Kerala’s agricultural sector, which has historically served as the fundamental edifice of the state’s socio-economic and cultural landscape, is currently navigating a period of profound systemic crisis2. An academic literature indicates that climate vulnerability needs to be identified at the regional level to inform agricultural, crop, and plantation policies.

The major threat from climate change in the agricultural sector is the erratic monsoon and weather patterns, which pose a significant threat to agricultural practices in Kerala. The possibility of threats to water availability, including drought and flooding, can also be a major concern. This crisis is multifaceted, on the one hand confronting environmental volatility created by climate change and, on the other, the deep social and economic shifts the state is undergoing. To effectively restore the state's agricultural sovereignty and long-term viability, the administration must acknowledge and address the widening decoupling of Kerala’s economic growth from its primary production3. It is imperative to confront the climate-induced stressors that are actively threatening the agricultural ecosystems upon which the state depends.

The structural shift in Kerala’s Gross State Domestic Product (GSDP) reflects a decisive move toward a service-oriented economy. This has happened at the direct expense of agricultural self- sufficiency. Data indicates that the share of agriculture toward the domestic product has fallen drastically from approximately 40% in 1980 to roughly 9% in recent years4. This agricultural marginalisation has increased the state’s reliance on food imports and eroded the rural economy’s inherent resilience. Simultaneously, the state’s ecological foundations are under unprecedented pressure, with a recorded mean annual temperature rise of 0.44°C5. This is not merely a statistical trend. This could threaten the Western Ghats, which have traditionally served as a vital carbon-sequestering canopy for the region6.

Understanding the current policy landscape

There has been emphasis upon the need for a more climate-specific and region-end approach in Kerala’s agriculture policy. This is evident from the Department of Agriculture, Government of Kerala, which is currently adopting an Agro-Ecological Units-based planning approach7. At present, Kerala is divided into 23 Agro-Ecological Units. Based on the soil temperature and other relevant geographic and climatic factors, crop selection and seed distribution are being maintained at the Agro-Ecological Units.

We can also consider that Kerala has initiated mechanisms to improve the value chain in the agriculture sector. The Kerala Climate Resilient Agri-Value Chain Modernisation (KERA) initiative, funded by the World Bank, is an important collaborative project that Kerala has undertaken. This initiative is supposed to help small-scale farmers with digital tools and infrastructure to adopt climate practices, improve production, and address any knowledge gaps.

Kerala’s agricultural sector is constrained by systemic policy gaps spanning land tenure, workforce retention, ecological management, and climate adaptation. Structurally, the absence of a progressive tenancy frameworks leaves 20% to 35% of cultivable land fallow under absentee landowners, while actual cultivators, disproportionately women and smallholders, are denied legal rights to access and utilize this land8. This insecurity of tenure is exacerbated by a broader labor policy deficit, where limited agrarian economic viability drives workforce attrition and out-migration among the employable-age population9 Concurrently, agricultural extension and land-use governance have failed to curb severe soil degradation stemming from decades of chemical overuse and deviations from ecosystem-based practices10. Finally, state agricultural policy lacks dedicated, microclimate-specific adaptation strategies to cushion heat-sensitive, high-value plantation crops from rising temperatures, leaving the sector acute to climate-induced shocks11.
