Project: Kerala Vision

Health

Despite Kerala’s high performance indicators that are no less favorable than those of developed countries, the state today faces a paradoxical set of problems: regular outbreaks of tropical and vector-borne infections, chronic lack of sanitation, and the inequalities in healthcare provision to tribal people, which expose structural gaps. Such structural weaknesses threaten higher-order effects to key sectors of the economy, especially tourism due to the loss of confidence of the populace and continuous strain on service delivery systems.

1. Disease Burden

The recurrence of tropical and vector-borne diseases in Kerala has emerged as a significant concern especially when frequent natural disasters aggravate transmission risks and hinder disease-control initiatives. The intersection of environmental and epidemiological threats presents substantial significance for the state's health security and developmental strategies.

a. Leptospirosis: Also known as rat fever, Leptospirosis is a major bacterial infection transmitted through contact with water or soil contaminated by infected animal urine, making flooded areas especially vulnerable1. The 2018 floods triggered an outbreak resulting in 2,079 confirmed cases and 99 confirmed deaths2. The threat worsened significantly in 2024, with reported cases reaching 5,980 (confirmed and probable) and 251 confirmed deaths3. By June 9, 2025, 1,451 cases and 74 deaths were reported, solidifying Leptospirosis as the leading cause of single-disease mortality in Kerala annually4.

b. Vector-Borne Diseases: Reflecting a state-wide surge in vector-borne infections—including malaria, Japanese encephalitis, and scrub typhus—dengue registered 20,674 confirmed cases (128 deaths) and 53,536 probable cases in Kerala during 20245.

c. Emerging Infectious Diseases: Beyond traditional tropical illnesses, Kerala's healthcare system faces escalating pressure from several emerging zoonotic and infectious pathogens. Nipah virus—first identified in Kozhikode (2018) and Ernakulam (2019)—causes recurring transmissions driven by habitat disruption, aerosolized bat secretions, and stress-induced viral shedding, prompting a shift toward community behavior modification over bat displacement6. West Nile virus, a mosquito-borne flavivirus circulating in a bird–mosquito cycle with incidental human hosts, emerged in Alappuzha in 2011 during an acute encephalitis syndrome outbreak (300 cases, 4 deaths)7, marking a broader shift in regional arboviral risk8. Additionally, Kyasanur Forest disease, a tick-borne haemorrhagic fever endemic to Karnataka, spread to Wayanad and Malappuram in 2014–15, causing 102 confirmed cases and 11 deaths in Wayanad in 20159. Lyme disease, a spirochete-caused infection historically rare in India10, was first recorded in Wayanad in 2013 (8 cases, 1 death)11 and later in Malappuram12. To strengthen institutional and community preparedness, Kerala is deploying initiatives such as Syndromic Surveillance for Acute Encephalitis13 and training teachers as first responders for snake encounters.

d. The Changing Face of Natural Disasters in Kerala: Heavy monsoon along with recurring flood events has resulted in stagnant water conditions that encourage the breeding of mosquitos, which leads to a substantial increase in dengue, malaria, and leptospirosis. Producing thousands of confirmed cases of leptospirosis and demonstrating how disease risk multiplies when infrastructure is disrupted, the 2018 floods show this trend. Persistent vulnerability was highlighted by the recent Wayanad landslides in July 2024. This vulnerability has been aggravated by anthropogenic climate change, where disastrous occurrences are increasing at an alarming rate.

2. Deteriorating Public Sanitation

In addition to infectious disease risks, Kerala's public sanitation infrastructure has been identified as a significant fault line. Testing of groundwater in ten districts has found alarming amounts of microbial contamination in nearly fifty percent of tested wells, which indicates failure of septic containment design and mismanagement of solid and liquid waste throughout Kerala. Additionally, tourist destinations on Kerala's coast (such as Fort Kochi) experience additional burdens from excessive plastic, poor waste processing capabilities, and resource limitations within local municipal governments.

a. Groundwater Contamination and Health Implications: Kerala's ongoing sanitation crisis poses severe public health threats through contaminated water and food infrastructure. Assessments by the Central Pollution Control Board’s National Water Quality Monitoring Programme found contamination in 16 of 31 sampled wells (~50%) across ten districts14. This contamination has driven major outbreaks, including a leptospirosis surge and a 2024 Hepatitis A outbreak that recorded 7,967 confirmed cases (89 deaths) and 20,445 probable cases (7 deaths)15. Simultaneously, contaminated food accounts for nearly half of all known disease outbreaks. Data from the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme shows that Kozhikode's food-borne disease rate jumped from 5.3% in 2022 to 10.1% in 202316. A survey of 2,000 local food handlers revealed significant occupational and infectious risks—including jaundice, typhoid, dermal infections, lacerations, and burns—with 7.4% reporting a history of jaundice and 1.4% a history of typhoid. Laboratory testing further showed 7.6% reactive to Salmonella typhi and 0.2% to the Hepatitis B surface antigen.

b. Lack of sanitation infrastructure: Poorly executed waste containment has heightened local contamination risks, prompting authorities to advise retrofitting inadequate systems with leak-proof alternatives, such as DRDO-developed bio-digesters and engineered septic tanks. Simultaneously, the state's healthcare infrastructure faces severe scrutiny following the fatal collapse of a bathroom block at Kottayam Medical College. The death of a caregiver in a facility declared unsafe over a decade ago sparked intense political backlash, with opposition parties condemning the state for forcing patients to rely on hazardous structures due to a lack of alternatives17.

c. Systemic Challenges and Out-of-Pocket Expenditure: According to National Health Accounts (NHA) 2021–22 data, Kerala leads India in both per capita government health spending (₹4,338) and total health expenditure (₹13,343)18. Paradoxically, the state also records the nation's highest per capita out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE) at ₹7,889 per year, which accounts for 59.1% of its total health spending. This heavy reliance on private care underscores significant quality gaps and poor sanitation standards in public hospitals. Furthermore, these persistent sanitation failures fuel recurring waterborne, vector-borne, and communicable disease outbreaks—straining healthcare infrastructure, diverting resources from long-term priorities, and revealing structural vulnerabilities in public health management.

3. Tribal Healthcare

Kerala’s tribal communities, concentrated in the rugged terrains of Wayanad, Idukki, Palakkad, and Attappady, face severe barriers to healthcare alongside persistent threats from wildlife attacks. Their systemic vulnerability was starkly exposed by the July Wayanad landslides, which resulted in 336 deaths and 397 casualties19. A significant proportion of the victims belonged to marginalized groups who lacked access to early warning systems and timely medical care.

a. Geographic Disadvantages to Access to Healthcare:

i. Infrastructure Deficits: Post-disaster disease risks disproportionately affect marginalized groups, such as agricultural laborers, fishermen, and shelter residents, who face high exposure to contaminated water and limited sanitation amenities despite the state's strong baseline health indicators.

ii. Access Issues: Remote tribal villages in the Western Ghats foothills lack all-weather roads, leaving communities entirely excluded from healthcare services during monsoons and making emergency medical evacuations nearly impossible.

iii. Facility Distribution: Severe manpower shortages and resource maldistribution strain public healthcare. For instance, Alappuzha Medical College Hospital faces critical vacancies (including 80 Assistant Professors, 10 Associate Professors, and two Professors) resulting in six-month waits for cardiac surgeries and inpatient loads exceeding bed capacity20.

b. Cultural and Socioeconomic Barriers:

i. Health-Seeking Behaviour: The tribal communities still adhere to their practice of traditional healing and only seek modern health care facilities in critical stages; there is poor assimilation between the old and new systems.

ii. Economic Limits: Outpatient care, medicines, and diagnostics account for a major share of Kerala's out-of-pocket health expenditure, as these expenses remain largely excluded from insurance coverage. Private outpatient visits are paid almost entirely out of pocket, creating a disproportionate financial burden on tribal communities with irregular incomes.

c. Disease Burden of the Tribal Areas: Emerging zoonotic infections reported in tribal-dominated regions (such as Kyasanur Forest disease and Lyme disease) require specialized treatment and surveillance protocols that remote healthcare facilities typically lack.

4. Effect of Healthcare on Tourism

a. Economic significance and role of public health in tourism:

Globally recognized for its natural heritage and wellness traditions, Kerala has leveraged its strong health infrastructure and traditional Ayurveda to become a premier medical and wellness tourism hub. In FY 2024–25, KeralaTourism.org outperformed the central government's Incredible India portal in web traffic, ranking second globally among travel sites21. By offering affordable, high-quality care that integrates modern medicine with traditional therapies, tourism has become a core driver of foreign exchange, employment, and state economic policy—making public health and environmental standards essential to sustaining Kerala's international competitiveness.

b. Medical- Tourism Nexus: Healthcare Quality as Economic Driver

The tourism sector currently produces an estimated generation of ₹40 crore monthly, and could produce ₹100 crore monthly22 as the state received ₹13,500.00 crore in healthcare and wellness tourism, which is 30 per cent of its total 45,000 crore tourism earnings in 202423. However, the potent healthcare challenges addressed above continue to threaten this economic potential.

Medical tourism has three pillars, which include clinical excellence, quality of infrastructure, and security of public health24. Kerala is a medical tourist destination and this status is demonstrated by the fact that the state hosted representatives of 18 countries during the Global Ayurveda Summit25 and being recognized for both modern medicine and traditional healing systems—depends critically on addressing systemic vulnerabilities.
