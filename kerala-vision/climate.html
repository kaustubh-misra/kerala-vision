Project: Kerala Vision

Climate

Kerala’s geographical position makes it extremely vulnerable to the climate crisis. The state consists of 40% of the Western Ghat that influence India's monsoon patterns, and the Arabian Sea that forms the entire Western boundary. Kerala is home to multiple endemic species. However, climate change comes as a central threat to Kerala and remains an urgent priority due to the state's unique geography and high population density that makes it vulnerable to extreme weather events, rising sea levels, and landslides. Rising sea levels and risk of landslides threaten livelihoods, ecosystems of the Western Ghats, cause soil and coastline erosion, and displace communities. Approximately 15% of the state’s landmass is prone to flooding1, and now coastal erosion affects 45% of Kerala's coastline2, threatening the livelihoods of fisheries and fishing communities.

Climate change also impacts Kerala’s economic life, specifically the tourism sector. Unpredictable monsoons, flash floods, and landslides - particularly in regions like Wayanad and Munnar - damage roads, resorts, and lead to travel advisories or extended closures. Between 8 and 19 August 2018, Kerala received 164% more rainfall than normal. During 14-19 August, the Keralan district of Idukki received the most rainfall (∼ 700 mm) - about twice the normal amount. The one- and two-day extreme precipitation values that occurred in Kerala on 15-16 August had return periods of 75 and 200 years respectively when compared to a long term record from 1901-20173. Periyar basin, one of the most affected areas, received a 145-year return period of rainfall.

In today’s climate, which is 1.3°C warmer than it would have been at the beginning of the industrial period, an event of this magnitude is expected to occur about once every 50 years. The event is the third heaviest 1-day rainfall event on record, with heavier spells in 2019 and in 1924, and surpasses the very heavy rainfall in 2018 that affected large regions of Kerala. While the linkage between land cover and land use changes and landslide risk in Wayanad is mixed in the limited existing studies, factors such as quarrying for building materials, and a 62% reduction in forest cover, may have contributed to the increased susceptibility of the slopes to landslides when the heavy rain fell4. The increase in climate change-driven rainfall found in this study is likely to increase the potential number of landslides that could be triggered in the future, raising the need for adaptation actions that may include the reinforcement of susceptible slopes, landslide early warning systems, and construction of retaining structures to protect vulnerable localities.

Similarly, due to coastline erosion, the Varkala cliffs and Kovalam beach are losing their shorelines, resulting in diminished appeal. Livelihoods are also undergoing displacement due to climate change induced migration. The case of Munroe island is an example of the same: Due to rising sea levels and ecological changes like saltwater intrusion into fertile lands, sustaining a livelihood has become difficult. Families relocate to nearby areas like Kollam city, abandoning their homes, and those that stay back face a lack of basic facilities like electricity and supply of drinking water. The spread of agricultural land all the way up the Western Ghats in Wayanad has been one of the primary reasons for the rainfall-driven landslides of 2018, 2019 & 2021. The lack of sustained systemic approaches to fix the Wayanad land has led to the loss of faith in the top-down efforts to manage Kerala’s increasingly bleak climate future.

The rising adverse effects of climate change globally are as evident in Kerala as elsewhere. The effects have complex, systemic impact on many aspects of societies and people, in addition to ecology, flora, and fauna. While this unfolds, Kerala may be facing another conundrum–brain drain of the youth5. If an increasing number of educated, capable, skilled, energetic youth are not focused on innovating and solving local problems and questions of sustainability, but are looking for opportunities elsewhere, that poses a systemic risk to society over the decades to come.
