Kerala · 2026 and beyond

Why Kerala?

Nestled between the vast Arabian Sea & the mighty Western Ghats, the scent of Malabar has attracted traders, priests & explorers to the resource-rich & geographically-blessed land of Kerala for over two-and-a half millennia. Geographically insulated from the rest of the Indian peninsula, Kerala historically looked outward to the sea, cultivating deep international ties through trade and migration. This exposure fostered an open social environment, which over time, reform movements channeled into a strong culture of rights, civic engagement, and public education. Progressively, these social foundations matured into the 'Kerala Model1': a distinct set of policies and institutions that delivers exceptional human development indicators despite modest economic growth.

What are the issues?

But in the last two-and-a-half decades, the gains from this model seem to be dissipating, majorly due to rejection of the post-1991 reforms leveraged by neighbouring Andhra, Karnataka & Tamil Nadu2: deregulated foreign direct investment to ignite a manufacturing and services boom, driving commercial technology adoption which increased labor productivity. Kerala charted its own path of a fast-growing remittance economy which kept consumption levels high3, which increased prices & wages, unsubstantiated by local industrial productivity4. Supported by a large political economy & labour movement, the increase in wages was sustained by the state by committing the majority of its revenues to recurring expenditure, such as salaries, pensions5, healthcare & subsidies6.

This divergent path now poses a defining test for Kerala’s future, bringing a central structural challenge into focus alongside several cascading effects:

1. The state has been engulfed by a deeper crisis of imagination, which has resulted in a collective failure to articulate a cohesive vision and forward-looking pathways for the growth of the state & its people. In turn, this deficit has incentivized Malayalis to leave their place to create an aspirational life for themselves elsewhere. This broader erosion of vision manifests itself in the following emergent ways:

a. While sitting on a base of strong public education system which could provide basic primary education to all its citizens, this advantage has not translated to a strong higher education system (technical or humanitarian) which could tap into the higher value market-led systems of industrial employment/entrepreneurship & ensure a higher standard of life, material & intellectual prosperity for those citizens who could afford aspiration of a better life for themselves.

b. The state’s fragile & rich ecology & biodiversity is undoubtedly one of its richest sources of collective commons & natural wealth. As the climate crisis grows worldwide, this thin fragile layer of a collective existence is under immense stress from increased population growth & search for greater value from livelihood sources. This manifested itself in many forms over the last decade, including natural calamities & public health incidences7, but has led to scarce care & concern by the state or its people, either in imagination, expression or action.

c. The loss of successive generations to out-migration has weakened the social bonds connecting youth to Kerala's land, culture, and heritage. Consequently, a globalized younger generation finds itself alienated from its roots and unintegrated into the state’s collective identity. This loss of social cohesion starves the local ecosystem of civic action, social entrepreneurship, and high-value innovation, giving young Malayalis little reason to stay.

This exodus manifests itself in the form of a bleak & hopeless future for Kerala. But it can be solved, by a collective, bottom-up vision which rebuilds the lost linkages of care & concern for the state by bringing people together & supporting their visions & actions through resources, connections & goodwill.
